What Martyn's Law is, in plain English

In 2017 a bomb at Manchester Arena killed 22 people. One of them was Martyn Hett. His mother campaigned for a law requiring venues to be prepared — and Parliament passed it in April 2025.

It asks places where crowds gather to know what they would do in the first few minutes of an emergency. Not to build fortifications. Not to hire guards. To have a plan, and to make sure the people on duty know it.

It only reaches your venue if 200 or more people could reasonably be there at the same time — counting your volunteers, at your busiest occasion of the year. Below that number, none of it applies to you.

If you are above it, you're in what's called the standard tier: the lighter set of duties, written so that a village hall committee can meet them without spending money on equipment. Places of worship stay in this lighter tier no matter how many people attend.

Straight answers

Questions volunteers actually ask

Does it apply to us?

We're a small church — surely this doesn't apply?
Run the free check above. If fewer than 200 people could reasonably be present at your busiest occasion (counting helpers), you're out of scope — and we'll email you a letter for your records saying so. Places of worship that are in scope stay in the lighter standard tier whatever their size.
Do we count volunteers, or just visitors?
Everyone present at the same time: visitors, staff, volunteers, performers, the people in the kitchen. Leaving helpers out of the count is the most common scoping mistake we see.
Our hall holds 300 but we never get more than 80. Are we in scope?
On that description, almost certainly not. The test is what could reasonably be expected to be present, not the theoretical capacity of the building. Write down the reasoning and your actual attendance figures, keep it with the fire log, and review it annually.
The hall is hired out — is it us or the hirer?
Generally whoever controls the premises for that use, which for a hall let is usually still the trustees or the council rather than the hirer. That's why we include a one-page emergency sheet for your hire pack — it puts the procedures in the hirer's hands without moving the duty off yours.

What we'd have to do

Do we need CCTV, barriers or security staff?
No. Standard-tier duties are procedures, not equipment. The Government's own guidance says most venues need no physical works at all — and warns against providers who claim otherwise.
Can't we just use our fire evacuation plan?
It's a foundation, not a substitute. In a fire, out is always right. In a terrorism scenario the safest action can be to stay in, or to avoid the usual exit. That difference is the point of the four procedures.
Is documentation legally required?
Honestly: at standard tier the Act requires the procedures to exist and work — it doesn't mandate paperwork. But written procedures are how you brief volunteers, hand over when the committee changes, satisfy your insurer and answer the regulator confidently. In practice everyone writes them down.
What happens if we do nothing?
From Spring 2027 the regulator can require compliance and issue penalties of up to £10,000 for standard-tier venues, with daily penalties for continuing breaches. The SIA has said it will be supportive and proportionate — especially with venues that have made a start. The expensive position is having nothing and no explanation.
